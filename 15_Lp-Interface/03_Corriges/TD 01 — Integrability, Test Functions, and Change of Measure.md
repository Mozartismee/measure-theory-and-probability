# Corrigé — TD 01: Integrability, Test Functions, and Change of Measure

## Exercise 1 — Finite-measure embeddings and their boundary

### 1. The case $q<+\infty$

Let $1\le p<q<+\infty$. Apply Hölder's inequality to

$$
|f|^p\mathbf1_E
$$

with conjugate exponents $q/p$ and $q/(q-p)$. One obtains

$$
\int_E|f|^p\,d\mu
\le
\left(\int_E|f|^q\,d\mu\right)^{p/q}
\mu(E)^{1-p/q}.
$$

Taking the $p$-th root gives

$$
\boxed{
\|f\|_p
\le
\mu(E)^{\frac1p-\frac1q}\|f\|_q
}.
$$

Hence $L^q(\mu)\subseteq L^p(\mu)$.

### 2. The case $q=+\infty$

Since $|f|\le\|f\|_\infty$ almost everywhere,

$$
\|f\|_p^p
\le
\|f\|_\infty^p\mu(E),
$$

and therefore

$$
\boxed{
\|f\|_p
\le
\mu(E)^{1/p}\|f\|_\infty
}.
$$

### 3. Counterexamples on an infinite measure space

Define

$$
f(x)
=
\frac{\mathbf1_{(0,e^{-1})}(x)}
{x\bigl(\log(1/x)\bigr)^2}.
$$

With $t=\log(1/x)$,

$$
\int_0^{e^{-1}}f(x)\,dx
=
\int_1^{+\infty}\frac{dt}{t^2}
<+\infty.
$$

For every $p>1$,

$$
\int_0^{e^{-1}}|f(x)|^p\,dx
=
\int_1^{+\infty}
\frac{e^{(p-1)t}}{t^{2p}}\,dt
=+\infty.
$$

Thus $f\in L^1(\lambda)$ but $f\notin L^p(\lambda)$ for every $p>1$.

Now fix $p>1$ and set

$$
a=\frac{p+1}{2p}.
$$

Then $a<1$ and $ap=(p+1)/2>1$. Hence

$$
g(x)=x^{-a}\mathbf1_{(1,+\infty)}(x)
$$

satisfies

$$
g\in L^p(\lambda),
\qquad
g\notin L^1(\lambda).
$$

### 4. Boundary

The finite-total-mass hypothesis $\mu(E)<+\infty$ is exactly what permits the constant function $\mathbf1_E$ to absorb the loss of exponent. On a general infinite measure space there is no inclusion between $L^p$ and $L^q$ merely from the order of the exponents.

## Exercise 2 — Indicators and bounded test functions

### 1. Endpoint estimate

For a representative satisfying $|Z|\le\|Z\|_\infty$ almost everywhere,

$$
|fZ|
\le
\|Z\|_\infty|f|.
$$

Therefore

$$
\|fZ\|_1
\le
\|Z\|_\infty\|f\|_1.
$$

### 2. Extension of the test class

The assumed identity holds for indicators $\mathbf1_A$, $A\in\mathcal G$. By linearity it holds for every $\mathcal G$-measurable simple function.

Let $Z$ be bounded and $\mathcal G$-measurable. Choose simple $\mathcal G$-measurable functions $Z_n$ such that

$$
Z_n\to Z
\quad\text{pointwise},
\qquad
|Z_n|\le\|Z\|_\infty.
$$

Then

$$
|Z_n(f-g)|
\le
\|Z\|_\infty|f-g|
\in L^1(\mu).
$$

Dominated convergence yields

$$
\int_E Z_n(f-g)\,d\mu
\longrightarrow
\int_E Z(f-g)\,d\mu.
$$

The left-hand side is zero for every $n$, hence so is the limit.

### 3. Why DCT

Neither $Z_n(f-g)$ nor its absolute value is required to increase with $n$; the functions may also change sign. Monotone convergence therefore has no legitimate object on which to act. What is available is a fixed integrable majorant, so the correct theorem is DCT.

### 4. Separation

Since $f$ and $g$ are $\mathcal G$-measurable, the sets

$$
A_+=\{f>g\},
\qquad
A_-=\{f<g\}
$$

belong to $\mathcal G$. The hypothesis gives

$$
\int_{A_+}(f-g)\,d\mu=0.
$$

The integrand is nonnegative and strictly positive on $A_+$, hence $\mu(A_+)=0$. Applying the same argument to $A_-$ gives $\mu(A_-)=0$. Thus $f=g$ almost everywhere.

## Exercise 3 — Measures generated by densities

### 1. Positive density

For pairwise disjoint $(A_n)$,

$$
\mathbf1_{\bigcup_n A_n}
=
\sum_{n\ge1}\mathbf1_{A_n}.
$$

The partial sums increase, so MCT gives

$$
\int_{\bigcup_n A_n}h\,d\mu
=
\sum_{n\ge1}\int_{A_n}h\,d\mu.
$$

Thus $h\mu$ is a positive measure. If $\mu(A)=0$, then $\int_Ah\,d\mu=0$, so $h\mu\ll\mu$.

### 2. Finiteness

The measure $h\mu$ is finite exactly when

$$
(h\mu)(E)=\int_Eh\,d\mu<+\infty,
$$

that is, when $h\in L^1_+(\mu)$.

### 3. Signed density

Write $f=f^+-f^-$. Since $f\in L^1(\mu)$, the measures $f^+\mu$ and $f^-\mu$ are finite. Therefore

$$
\nu_f=f^+\mu-f^-\mu
$$

is a finite signed measure.

### 4. Canonical decomposition

On $P=\{f\ge0\}$ every measurable restriction has nonnegative integral; hence $P$ is positive for $\nu_f$. Similarly, $N=\{f<0\}$ is negative. Thus $(P,N)$ is a Hahn decomposition, and

$$
\boxed{
\nu_f^+=f^+\mu,
\qquad
\nu_f^-=f^-\mu,
\qquad
|\nu_f|=|f|\mu
}.
$$

### 5. Uniqueness of densities

Set $w=f-g$. From $f\mu=g\mu$ one gets

$$
\int_Aw\,d\mu=0,
\qquad A\in\mathcal A.
$$

Taking $A=\{w>0\}$ and then $A=\{w<0\}$ shows that both sets are null. Hence $w=0$ almost everywhere.

## Exercise 4 — Change of measure and equivalence classes

### 1. Integral under a density

For indicators the formula

$$
\int_E\mathbf1_A\,d\nu
=
\int_E\mathbf1_Ah\,d\mu
$$

is the definition of $\nu=h\mu$. Linearity gives it for nonnegative simple functions, and MCT gives it for all nonnegative measurable functions. Applying the result to $f^+$ and $f^-$ yields

$$
f\in L^1(\nu)
\Longleftrightarrow
\int_E|f|h\,d\mu<+\infty,
$$

and, in that case,

$$
\int_Ef\,d\nu
=
\int_Efh\,d\mu.
$$

### 2. Bounded density

If $h\le C$ almost everywhere, then

$$
\|f\|_{L^1(\nu)}
=
\int_E|f|h\,d\mu
\le
C\|f\|_{L^1(\mu)}.
$$

Thus $L^1(\mu)\hookrightarrow L^1(\nu)$ continuously.

### 3. Absolute continuity is not an integrability estimate

Let $E=(0,1)$, let $\mu=\lambda$, and define

$$
h(x)=x^{-1/2},
\qquad
\nu=h\mu.
$$

Both $\mu$ and $\nu$ are finite, and $\nu\ll\mu$. However, with $f(x)=x^{-1/2}$,

$$
\int_0^1|f|\,d\mu=2<+\infty,
$$

whereas

$$
\int_0^1|f|\,d\nu
=
\int_0^1\frac{dx}{x}
=+\infty.
$$

Therefore $L^1(\mu)\not\subseteq L^1(\nu)$.

### 4. Two distinct checks

To define

$$
[f]_\mu\longmapsto[f]_\nu,
$$

one needs:

1. integrability transfer: $f\in L^1(\mu)$ implies $f\in L^1(\nu)$;
2. compatibility of representatives: $f=g$ $\mu$-almost everywhere implies $f=g$ $\nu$-almost everywhere.

The first follows from a quantitative domination such as $\nu\le C\mu$. The second follows from the qualitative relation $\nu\ll\mu$. They are not the same assertion.

### 5. Equivalent measures

We already know $\nu\ll\mu$. If $\nu(A)=0$, then

$$
\int_Ah\,d\mu=0.
$$

Since $h>0$ almost everywhere, this implies $\mu(A)=0$. Hence $\mu\ll\nu$, so $\mu$ and $\nu$ have the same null sets.

## Reconstruction — Proof spine

The stable chain is

$$
|fZ|\le\|Z\|_\infty|f|
\Longrightarrow
fZ\in L^1,
$$

$$
\text{indicators}
\xrightarrow{\text{linearity}}
\text{simple tests}
\xrightarrow{\text{DCT}}
\text{bounded tests},
$$

$$
f=f^+-f^-
\Longrightarrow
f\mu=f^+\mu-f^-\mu,
$$

and

$$
\int_A(f-g)\,d\mu=0\ \forall A
\Longrightarrow
\mu(f>g)=\mu(f<g)=0.
$$

Finally, $\nu\ll\mu$ transfers null sets. Integrability requires a bound on the size of $d\nu/d\mu$, not merely its existence.
